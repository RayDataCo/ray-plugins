# Window-Dressing Patterns and Covenant Mechanics

Read this when applying Step 5 (covenant headroom) or Step 7 (window-dressing awareness) in depth — beyond naming the gate, this is the detail behind why it exists and how far it can legitimately be pushed.

## Period-end window-dressing patterns

The ratio battery is a snapshot, and snapshots taken at a period-end are structurally vulnerable to short-term actions timed specifically around the reporting date. None of these are illegal — they're timing choices — but they mean a single period-end figure can flatter the picture in ways that reverse days later. Common patterns:

- **Revolver paydown-then-redraw** — draw down the revolver to pay off other current liabilities or build cash right before period-end, improving the current ratio and available-liquidity snapshot, then redraw shortly after the reporting date once the figure has been captured. The company's actual average liquidity position across the period didn't change; only the snapshot did.
- **Accelerated collections / delayed payables right at period-end** — pushing customers to pay early (sometimes via early-payment discounts) or intentionally slow-walking vendor payments in the days immediately around period-end. This temporarily depresses AR and inflates AP (both improving CCC-adjacent metrics) in a way that reverses in the following days.
- **Receivables factoring shortly before period-end** — selling receivables to a factor converts AR to cash right before the snapshot, improving the cash ratio and cash conversion metrics, without reflecting a durable change in the underlying collection cycle.

**The defense is trend discipline, not single-period trust.** None of these patterns are visible from one snapshot — a paydown-then-redraw looks identical to a genuine deleveraging if you only have the period-end number. What reveals it: a metric that improves sharply right at a reporting date and reverts (or the revolver balance jumps back up) shortly after, visible only across a sequence of snapshots. Step 7 in `SKILL.md` scopes this correctly as an **awareness/flagging gate**, not a detection algorithm — the skill doesn't have access to intra-period (daily/weekly) balances a typical ask supplies, so it cannot prove manipulation occurred. What it can do: flag when a metric's period-end improvement looks sharp relative to the trend on either side of it, and recommend scrutiny (ask what the balance looked like a few weeks before/after the reporting date) rather than crediting the number automatically.

**Do not overclaim.** A statement like "this current ratio was likely window-dressed" is not supportable from period-end snapshots alone unless the pattern (sharp move right at the reporting date, reversion visible in the next period's opening position) is actually present in the given data. The correct framing is conditional: "a metric this X at exactly the reporting date deserves scrutiny before being credited as a durable improvement" — not an accusation.

## Covenant cure mechanics

A covenant breach at a test date is not automatically an event of default with immediate consequences — most credit agreements build in mechanisms that mean the *technical* distance to a covenant threshold is not the same as the *forced-outcome* distance (acceleration, cross-default, loss of facility access). Understanding these matters for correctly framing what a thin headroom number actually means:

- **Cure periods** — many agreements give the borrower a defined window (often 10–30 business days) after a breach is identified to remedy it before it becomes an event of default. A breach discovered at quarter-end doesn't necessarily mean immediate consequences if it can be cured within the window.
- **Equity cures** — some agreements (more common in sponsor-backed / leveraged deals) allow the equity sponsor to inject cash specifically designated to be added back to EBITDA (or used to pay down debt) for covenant-calculation purposes, curing a covenant breach without the underlying operating performance actually improving. This is a contractual mechanism, not an operating fix — worth noting explicitly if headroom is thin and the deal structure suggests an equity-cure right may exist, since it changes the practical severity of a projected breach.
- **Waivers and amendments** — lenders can (and frequently do, for an otherwise-performing borrower with a covenant-technical issue) waive a specific breach or amend the covenant level going forward, usually for a fee and/or pricing step-up. A thin or breached headroom figure computed from stated terms is the correct output for this skill (it should never be silently smoothed over), but the caveat that lenders have discretion to waive is worth surfacing if the analysis is being used to gauge real-world severity rather than pure covenant-compliance mechanics.

**What this means for the headroom output:** compute and report the ratio-form and both dollar-form headroom figures exactly as specified in Step 5 — that's the mechanical answer the skill is scoped to produce, and it should never be softened by an assumption that "the bank will probably waive it anyway." But if the computed headroom is thin (approaching zero) or breached, it's appropriate to note, as a caveat rather than a computed figure, that the practical severity depends on cure-period timing, any equity-cure right in the specific agreement, and lender relationship/waiver history — none of which this skill computes, since covenant cure terms are part of the credit agreement's own specific language, not a generic default this skill should assume.
